According to the United Nations World Water Development Report 2025, approximately 2.2 billion people worldwide lack access to safely managed drinking water services, while 4.2 billion people — more than half the global population — live without safely managed sanitation. Experts argue that addressing the water crisis requires a multi-pronged approach: investment in infrastructure, policy reform to regulate industrial pollution, community-led conservation initiatives, and international cooperation to manage transboundary water resources. Without urgent action, the World Bank estimates that water scarcity could reduce GDP growth by up to 6 percent in some regions by 2050.

### Passage 2 — Argumentative / Opinion-Based (Questions 6–10)

The rapid proliferation of artificial intelligence in educational settings has sparked a vigorous debate among educators, technologists, and policymakers. Proponents argue that AI-powered tools — such as intelligent tutoring systems, automated essay graders, and adaptive learning platforms — have the potential to democratise education by personalising instruction for millions of students who lack access to quality teaching. In under-resourced schools across the developing world, where a single teacher may be responsible for eighty or more students, AI could bridge critical gaps by providing individualised feedback and pacing.

However, critics raise legitimate concerns that deserve careful examination. First, there is the issue of algorithmic bias. AI systems trained on data that reflects existing social inequalities may inadvertently perpetuate those very disparities. An automated essay grader, for instance, might systematically undervalue non-standard dialects or culturally diverse modes of expression, thereby disadvantaging students from marginalised communities. Second, over-reliance on AI threatens to erode the fundamentally human dimensions of education — empathy, mentorship, moral guidance, and the cultivation of critical thinking through Socratic dialogue. A machine can assess whether an answer is correct; it cannot inspire a student to question why the question matters in the first place.

Furthermore, the data privacy implications are staggering. AI-driven educational platforms collect vast quantities of sensitive information about minors — their learning habits, cognitive strengths and weaknesses, behavioural patterns, and even emotional states. Without robust regulatory frameworks, this data is vulnerable to commercial exploitation by technology companies whose primary obligation is to shareholders rather than students.

The most balanced approach lies neither in wholesale adoption nor reflexive rejection of AI in education. Instead, schools should integrate these tools as supplements to, rather than substitutes for, skilled human educators. Policymakers must establish clear ethical guidelines, mandate transparency in algorithmic decision-making, and ensure that the deployment of AI in classrooms is guided by pedagogical evidence rather than corporate marketing. Technology should serve education — not the other way around.

The monsoon arrived not with the gentle persuasion of a guest but with the imperious certainty of a conqueror. It came first as a scent — the intoxicating fragrance of parched earth receiving its first benediction of rain, what the villagers called *petrichor*, though they knew it by an older, nameless intimacy. Then came the wind, sweeping across the mango orchards with a restlessness that set every leaf trembling in anticipation, as though the trees themselves understood that something magnificent and inevitable was about to unfold.

Kamala stood at the threshold of her grandmother's house, one foot on the cool, worn stone of the veranda and the other on the warm dust of the courtyard. She was, in that small physical gesture, poised between two worlds — the sheltered interior with its smell of sandalwood and turmeric, its brass lamps flickering against walls darkened by decades of wood smoke, and the vast, open theatre of the sky, where clouds the colour of bruised plums were assembling with theatrical urgency. Her grandmother, Ajji, sat behind her on a low wooden stool, oiling her silver hair with coconut oil, humming a song that seemed to have no beginning and no end, a melody that existed outside the tyranny of time.

The first drops fell like coins — heavy, singular, deliberate — each one exploding against the dust with a tiny crater of its own making. Within minutes, the singular became a multitude, and the multitude became a roar. The courtyard transformed into a shallow, shimmering lake that reflected the dark sky above. Kamala stepped forward, and the rain received her as it received everything — without judgment, without preference, with the absolute, indiscriminate generosity of nature. She tilted her face upward and felt each drop as a small, cold punctuation mark against her skin, as though the sky were writing a message she could feel but not yet read.

Behind her, Ajji continued to hum. The song and the rain became indistinguishable, woven into a single fabric of sound that wrapped around the old house like a shawl. In that moment, the distance between memory and experience, between the old woman and the young girl, between the past and the present, collapsed into something luminous and whole.
